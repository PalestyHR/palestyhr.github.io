My Journey · Background Story

Finding my direction

I once thought my future would have something to do with the sky.

As a child, I wanted to become a pilot or an astronaut. I did not really know what either profession involved, but the thought of flying far away and seeing a world I had never seen before excited me. That childhood dream still influenced me when I chose aviation engineering. I hoped that studying a subject connected to aviation would bring me closer to the future I had imagined.

Only after entering university did I begin to realize that being fascinated by flight and enjoying aviation engineering were two different things. I had seen the side of aviation that inspired me without really understanding what studying and working in the field would look like day to day. As those realities became the courses in front of me, I struggled to connect them with the future I had hoped for.

The gap left me disappointed. I kept wondering whether I had mistaken a longing for a certain kind of life for an interest in a field of study. Would I have chosen differently if I had understood more beforehand? Yet whenever I imagined another path, I could not quite say where I wanted to go. I knew what felt wrong, but not what I wanted to devote myself to.

Around that time, I often talked with AI. Sometimes I wanted help with studying or thinking about my future; at other times, I simply wanted to put the tangled thoughts in my head into words. Those responses sometimes made me feel understood. A model could follow what I was saying, help me organize my thoughts, and respond to feelings I struggled to express.

As I learned how language models generate their responses, that feeling became more complicated. If a model does not experience emotions and life in the way a person does, why could its responses still make me feel understood? What was it picking up from my words, including things I had left unsaid?

These were not purely technical questions for me. I had found real comfort in those conversations, and learning about the mechanisms behind them brought uncertainty as well as curiosity. I wanted to understand what was happening inside the models: how they connect information, display abilities that look like understanding and reasoning, and sometimes fail at things that seem simple to us. I was no longer only looking to AI for answers. I also wanted to understand the systems producing them.

A later robotics internship gave that interest something more tangible to focus on. Working with real hardware and software made me wonder what would happen if this kind of intelligence had a body. How would it perceive its surroundings and turn perception into judgment and action? Its response could be more than text—it could be a physical action with consequences in the world.

I began thinking about what it would mean for these machines to live alongside us. We do not always express our needs clearly or behave as a machine might expect. What would a robot need to understand to become a helpful assistant? How should it respond when information is incomplete or a person's intentions are unclear?

I tried to turn some of these observations into research questions, moving between reading, implementation, and experiments. Some results did not match my expectations; some ideas turned out not to work. I still questioned my own abilities.

But I noticed that I wanted to keep going. When an experiment failed, I wanted to know why. When an idea reached a dead end, I looked for another explanation. No course required me to pursue these questions, and no one could promise that the time I spent would lead to anything. Still, I found satisfaction in choosing to investigate something I cared about.

That was an important change for me. I had spent so much time looking back and questioning my choices. Now there were also things I looked forward to exploring.

Today, I want to understand how models develop their capabilities and how those capabilities connect with language, vision, action, and the physical world. Part of that interest has always been about people. Having once looked for understanding in conversations with AI, I hope to explore how AI and robots can respond to people's needs, emotions, and actions in natural, considerate ways.

I do not yet have a complete answer about my future, and I have not entirely left behind the uncertainty surrounding my choices. But I have found questions I am willing to keep working on, even when their answers are not simple.

As a child, I was drawn to the sky because there was something unknown to explore. Today, that curiosity is also finding its way toward intelligence, understanding, and the relationship between people and machines.

Research Beginnings

From practice to research

My research experience began during a robotics internship at Lens Technology, where I worked as a Mechanical Engineering Intern under the mentorship of Dr. Changchun Zhou. We remain in close contact. During the internship, I gained hands-on experience in both hardware and software, contributing to dexterous-hand mechanical design, performance testing, and robot assembly, as well as algorithm development for visual tracking and robot perception.

The internship also gave me opportunities to turn practical observations into research questions. One example is P-SRM, which grew out of my search for a visual tracking algorithm for the company’s badminton robot. While reproducing an existing tracker, I noticed that its rejection mechanism could discard correctly localized predictions. This observation led me to investigate whether these rejected predictions could be selectively recovered, eventually developing the idea into P-SRM.
